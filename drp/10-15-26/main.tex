\section{Tropical Hypersurfaces and Newton Polytopes}

\subsection{Tropical Hypersurfaces}

A tropical polynomial in $n$ variables can be written as
\[f(\xx)=\bigoplus_{\ii \in S}a_{\ii}\odot \xx^\ii\]
where $S\subseteq \zz_{\ge 0}^n$ is finite, $a_i\in \rr$, and 
\[\xx^{\ii}=x_1^{i_1}\odot x_2^{i_2}\odot\cdots \odot x_n^{i_n}.\]
As an ordinary function $f:\rr^n \to \rr$,
\[f(\xx)=\min_{\ii \in S}(a_\ii+\langle \ii,\xx\rangle ).\]

\begin{defin}
    The tropical hypersurface $V(f)$ is the set of points at which the minimum defining $f$ is attained by at least two distinct monomials. Specifically,
    \[V(f)=\{\xx \in \rr^n:|\argmin_{\ii \in S}(c_\ii+\langle \ii,\xx\rangle )\ge 2\}.\]

    Equivalently, $V(f)$ is the locus where $f$ is not locally linear. 

    For $n=2$, $V(f)$ is a tropical curve. In general, a tropical hypersurface is a polyhedral complex of dimension $n-1$.
\end{defin}

Suppose two polynomials
\[f_1(\xx)=\langle \ii_1,\xx\rangle+a_1,\qquad f_2(\xx)=\langle \ii_2,\xx\rangle +a_2\]
attain the minimum simultaneously. The two equations imply
\[\langle\ii_1-\ii_2,\xx\rangle=a_2-a_1.\]
This defines a hyperplane whose normal vector is $\ii_1-\ii_2$. Therefore, any codimension-one cell on which precisely these two monomials attain the minimum lies in a hyperplane perpendicular to this vector joining their exponent vectors.

The geometry of the tropical hypersurface is controlled by differences between the exponent vectors of its monomials. 

We can make this correspondence precise using Newton polytopes.

\subsection{Newton Polytopes and Regular Subdivisions}

\begin{defin}
    The \textit{Newton polytope} of $f$ is
    \[\Delta_f=\conv (S)\subseteq \rr^n.\]

    It is the convex hull of the exponent vectors of the monomials in $f$.
\end{defin}

Note that $\Delta_f$ only depends on the exponents, while $V(f)$ depends on both the exponents and the coefficients. To incorporate the coefficients, we lift each exponent vector into one higher dimension.

\begin{defin}
    Define the lifted point configuration
    \[\tilde S=\{(\ii,a_\ii):\ii \in S\}\subseteq \rr^{n+1}.\]

    Denote its upper convex hull as
    \[\tilde{\Delta}_f=\conv (\tilde S).\]
\end{defin}

\begin{defin}
    The \textit{regular subdivision} $P_f$ of $\Delta_f$ is obtained by projecting the bottom faces of $\tilde{\Delta}_f$ onto $\rr^n$. 

    These projected faces from a polyhedral subdivision of $\Delta_f$. 
\end{defin}

\begin{rmk}
    We use the bottom faces because tropical addition is defined using minimum, and vertices on the bottom faces correspond to monomials that can attain their minimum for some $\xx$. Any interior point of $\tilde{\Delta}(f)$, on the other hand, will never attain its minimum, hence removing them will not change the polynomial output.

    We can consider hyperplanes of different slopes approaching $\tilde{\Delta}(f)$ from below, and their first intersections with $\tilde{\Delta}(f)$ exactly correspond to the monomials attaining their minimum. An intersection need not be a point, in which case multiple monomials attain their minimum at the same time.
\end{rmk}

\subsection{Duality}

The tropical polynomial $f$ partitions $\rr^n$ into polyhedral regions according to which monomials attain the minimum. Let $Q_f$ denote the corresponding polyhedral complex, including its faces. Its codimension-one skeleton is the tropical hypersurface $V(f)$.

\begin{thm}
    Let $f$ be a tropical polynomial in $n$ variables, and assume its Newton polytope $\Delta_f$ has dimension $n$. 

    There is an inclusion-reversing correspondence between the cells of the regular subdivision $P_f$ and the cells of $Q_f$ in the following way:

    If
    \[\tau \in P_f \quad \longleftrightarrow \quad \check\tau \in Q_f,\]
    then
    \[\dim \tau + \dim \check\tau = n.\]

    Also,
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The corresponding $\tau$ and $\check\tau$ are orthogonal;
        \item the exponent vectors correspondint to monomials attaining the minimum on the interior of $\check\tau$ have convex hull $\tau$.
    \end{itemize}

    For $n=2$, 
    \[\text{vertex} \longleftrightarrow \text{region},\quad \text{edge}\longleftrightarrow \text{edge},\quad \text{polygon}\longleftrightarrow\text{vertex}.\]

    In particular, an edge of a tropical curve is perpendicular to its dual edge in the Newton subdivision.
\end{thm}

We will work out the correspondence fully in $n=2$ case, and the same approach generalizes to higher dimensions.

\begin{prop}
    Let $f$ be a tropical polynomial in $2$ variables, and assume its Newton polytope $\Delta_f$ has dimension $2$.

    Let $\varphi(i_1,i_2)$ denote the minimum (equivalently, tropical sum) of coefficients of all monomials with exponents $(i_1,i_2)$.
    
    There is a bijection between subdivisions $P_f$ of $\Delta_f$ and $Q_f$ of $\rr^2$ by $\tau \mapsto \check\tau$ where 
    \[
    \check\tau = \left\{(m_1,m_2) \in \rr^2 \Biggm|
    \begin{aligned}
        \exists a \in \rr \text{ s.t. } 
        \forall (t_1,t_2) \in \tau,~-m_1t_1-m_2t_2+a & =\varphi(t_1,t_2), \\
        \forall (i_1,i_2) \in \Delta_f,~-m_1t_1-m_2t+a &\le \varphi(i_1,i_2).
    \end{aligned}
    \right\}
    \]
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch.]
    We interpret the equations for $P_f$ and $Q_f$ respectively.

    We first consider the case where $\tau=(t_1,t_2)$ is a point, then $\check\tau$ is $2$-dimensional.
    
    For $P_f$, $-\mathbf m$ is the gradient of a plane which goes through $(t_1,t_2,\varphi(t_1,t_2))$ and below $\tilde\Delta f.$ 

    For $Q_f$, the equations can be rewritten as
    \[m_1t_1+m_2t_2+\varphi(t_1,t_2)=a\]
    and
    \[a \le m_1i_1+m_2i_2+\varphi(i_1,i_2).\]
    Since $(i_1,i_2) \in S$ and $\varphi(i_1,i_2)$ is the coefficient for a monomial,
    \[m_1i_1+m_2i_2+\varphi(i_1,i_2)=a_{(i_1,i_2)}\otimes x_1^{i_1}\otimes x_2^{i_2}=a_\ii \xx^\ii\]
    in tropical arithmetic.

    Hence, $\check\tau$ is the domain of linearity where $a_\tau\xx^\tau$ attains minimum. 

    Then, suppose $\tau$ is an edge between $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$. Then,
    \[\tau=\text{edge between } \tau_1\text{ and }\tau_2 \quad \longleftrightarrow \quad \check \tau_1\cap \check\tau_2.\]

    We can also see the correspondence clearly: a plane that goes through both $(\tau_1,\varphi(\tau_1))$ and $(\tau_2,\varphi(\tau_2))$ represents both monomials of $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$ attains their minimum. 

    For higher dimensions, we can reason by induction.
\end{proof}
