\section{Motivations}

Our goal is to use geometry to visualize bidders' preferences.
The following example illustrates how a bidder's demand changes
across price space.

\begin{example} \label{example:sunny-rainy}
    A hotel offers two rooms, labeled 1 and 2, at prices $p_1$ and $p_2$,
    respectively. Sunny wants at most one room. His utility at the possible
    bundles is
    \[
        U_{\text{Sunny}}(0,0)=0,\quad
        U_{\text{Sunny}}(1,0)=30-p_1,\quad
        U_{\text{Sunny}}(0,1)=40-p_2.
    \]

    Thus, Sunny's demand is determined by the largest of the three affine
    functions $0$, $30-p_1$, and $40-p_2$. Figure~\ref{fig:sunny-demand}
    shows the resulting subdivision of the nonnegative price orthant.

    \SunnyDemandFigure

    Note that the two rooms are substitutes for Sunny.

    Rainy, by contrast, wants either both rooms or neither room. Her utility is
    \[
        U_{\text{Rainy}}(0,0)=0,\quad
        U_{\text{Rainy}}(1,1)=50-p_1-p_2.
    \]

    Rainy buys both rooms precisely when $p_1+p_2<50$ and buys neither
    when $p_1+p_2>50$. Her demand is therefore separated by a single
    indifference line, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rainy-demand}.

    \RainyDemandFigure

    Note that the two rooms are complements for Rainy.
\end{example}

We say that a \textit{competitive equilibrium} exists at a price vector $p$ if
the entire supply can be allocated so that every agent receives a
utility-maximizing bundle at $p$.

\begin{example}
    We can visualize Sunny's and Rainy's demands in
    Example~\ref{example:sunny-rainy} on a single graph by superposing their demand
    complexes, as in Figure~\ref{fig:combined-demand}.

    \CombinedDemandFigure
    
    Since the supply is one copy of each room, $s=(1,1)$, a market-clearing
    price would require aggregate demand $(1,1)$. Figure~\ref{fig:combined-demand}
    has no region with this label, and none of the boundary demand sets
    contains $(1,1)$. Hence, no competitive equilibrium exists.

    Now suppose that Rainy values the pair of rooms more highly. Her updated
    utility is
    \[
        U'_{\text{Rainy}}(0,0)=0,\quad
        U'_{\text{Rainy}}(1,1)=80-p_1-p_2.
    \]

    Figure~\ref{fig:updated-combined-demand} retains Rainy's original
    indifference line as a dashed reference and adds her updated indifference
    line.

    \UpdatedCombinedDemandFigure

    A competitive equilibrium exists at every price vector in the shaded
    triangle. Indeed, this region is defined by
    $p_1\ge 30$, $p_2\ge 40$, and $p_1+p_2\le 80$: Sunny demands no room,
    while Rainy demands both.
\end{example}

The rest of these notes develops the geometry underlying these pictures.
We first study the geometry generated by a single agent, then understand
how these geometries combine across agents, and finally use this structure
to study existence and counting of competitive equilibria.

\section{Demand Geometry of a Single Agent}

\subsection{Locus of indifference prices}

Let $J$ be the set of agents. For each $j \in J$, let
$X^j \subseteq \zz^n$ be the set of bundles available to agent $j$. 
The agent's valuation is a function $V^j\colon X^j \to \rr$, 
and the corresponding quasi-linear utility of bundle $x$ 
at price $p\in\rr^n$ is $V^j(x)-p\cdot x$. 
Thus, agent $j$ demands bundles in
\[
    D^j(p)=\argmax_{x \in X^j}\{V^j(x)-p\cdot x\}.
\]

For a fixed bundle $x$, the function $V^j(x)-p\cdot x$ is affine-linear in 
$p$. Consequently, price space is subdivided into regions according to which
of these affine functions attain the maximum.

In this notation, competitive equilibrium can be defined as follows.

\begin{defin}
    Given a supply vector $s$, a \textit{competitive equilibrium} consists of
    a price vector $p$ and bundles $(x^j)_{j\in J}$ such that
    \[
        x^j \in D^j(p) \quad\text{for every } j \in J,
    \]
    and
    \[
        \sum_{j \in J}x^j=s.
    \]
\end{defin}

\begin{defin}
    The \textit{locus of indifference prices} (LIP) for agent $j$ is the set
    of prices at which the agent demands more than one bundle:
    \[
        L^j = \{p \in \rr^n : |D^j(p)|>1\}.
    \]

    The linear pieces in Figures~\ref{fig:sunny-demand}
    and~\ref{fig:rainy-demand} are the corresponding LIP prices.

    We call an $(n-1)$-dimensional piece of the LIP a \textit{facet}, where the agent is indifferent between exactly two bundles.
\end{defin}

Suppose two distinct bundles $x,y$ are demanded at the same
price $p$. Then
\[
    V^j(x)-p\cdot x=V^j(y)-p\cdot y,
\]
which implies
\[
    p\cdot(x-y)=V^j(x)-V^j(y).
\]

Thus, prices at which $x$ and $y$ are indifferent lie
on a hyperplane orthogonal to $x-y$.

In particular, an edge of the demand complex is
perpendicular to its corresponding facet of the LIP.

In fact, we can make the following definition to get the precise change across any facet.

\begin{defin}
    Let $F$ be a facet of the LIP, separating two
    regions with unique demanded bundles $x$ and $y$.

    We can write
    \[
        x-y = w(F)v_F,
    \]
    where $v_F\in\zz^n$ is a primitive integer 
    (coordinates have greatest common divisor 1)
    normal vector to $F$, and $w(F)\in\zz$.

    We call $w(F)$ the \textbf{weight} of $F$.
\end{defin}

Intuitively, the weight measures how many units of
demand change across a facet, in its primitive direction.

\begin{defin}
    Let $(\Pi,w)$ be an $(n-1)$-dimensional weighted
    rational polyhedral complex in $\rr^n$.

    For every codimension-two cell $G$, let
    $F_1,\dots,F_k$ be the facets containing $G$.
    Choose primitive integer normal vectors $v_i$
    to $F_i$, consistently oriented around $G$.

    The complex is \textbf{balanced} if
    \[
        \sum_{i=1}^k w(F_i)v_i=0
    \]
    at every such cell $G$.

    Intuitively, suppose one starts from a point (not on the LIP) and travels around in a loop. Whenever the loop meets a facet, the demanded bundle changes according to the weight. When one returns back to the original point, the demanded bundle should not change, i.e., the cumulative net change is zero.
\end{defin}

% TODO: a figure of traveling around a loop

\begin{thm}[Balancing criteria]
    Let $(\Pi,w)$ be a pure $(n-1)$-dimensional
    positively weighted rational polyhedral complex
    in $\rr^n$, with positive integer weights.

    Then $(\Pi,w)$ is the weighted LIP of some
    valuation on a finite subset of $\zz^n$
    if and only if $(\Pi,w)$ is balanced.
\end{thm}

\subsection{Demand Complex}

The LIP describes an agent's preferences in \textbf{price space}.
We now construct a dual object in \textbf{quantity space}, whose
coordinates represent bundles of goods.

\begin{defin}
    Let $j$ be an agent with demand correspondence $D_j(p)$.
    The \textbf{demand complex} of agent $j$ is
    \[
        \Sigma_j=\left\{\operatorname{conv}(D_j(p)):p\in\rr^n\right\}.
    \]
    
    Its cells are the convex hulls of bundles demanded 
    together at some price.
\end{defin}

The demand complex is a polyhedral complex. Its vertices
are $0$-dimensional cells, its edges are $1$-dimensional cells,
and more generally its $k$-cells have dimension $k$.

Intuitively, the demand complex records which bundles can be
simultaneously optimal, without recording the prices at which
this happens.

\begin{example}
    Recall Sunny and Rainy from Example~\ref{example:sunny-rainy}.

    Sunny may demand $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, or $(0,1)$.
    At $p=(30,40)$, all three bundles are equally optimal.
    Therefore, his demand complex contains the triangle
    \[
        \operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\},
    \]
    together with all its faces.

    Rainy may demand only $(0,0)$ or $(1,1)$.
    At prices satisfying $p_1+p_2=50$, both are optimal.
    Her demand complex is the line segment
    \[
        \operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),(1,1)\},
    \]
    together with its endpoints.

    % Figure: Sunny's triangular demand complex
    % and Rainy's diagonal line segment.
\end{example}

\subsection{Duality Between LIP and Demand Complex}

The LIP and demand complex encode the same geometric
structure from two different perspectives.

\begin{thm}
    For a valuation on finitely many bundles, there is a
    correspondence between the cells of its demand complex 
    and its price complex:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Vertices of the demand complex correspond to
        closures of regions of unique demand.
        \item Edges of the demand complex correspond to
        facets of the LIP.
        \item More generally, a $k$-dimensional demand cell
        corresponds to an $(n-k)$-dimensional price cell.
    \end{itemize}
    
    Corresponding cells are orthogonal, and inclusion
    relations are reversed.
\end{thm}

\begin{example}
    Consider Sunny's demand complex.

    Its three vertices correspond to the three regions of
    unique demand in price space.

    Its three edges correspond to the three facets of his LIP.
    For example, the edge joining $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ has
    direction $(1,-1)$, perpendicular to the indifference
    line
    \[
        p_2-p_1=10.
    \]

    Finally, the triangular $2$-cell corresponds to the
    price $p=(30,40)$, where all three bundles are demanded.

    % Figure: LIP and demand complex side by side,
    % highlighting the corresponding cells.
\end{example}

\begin{rmk}
    The additive inverse of the actual utility function
    \[
        -F_j(p)=-\max_{x\in X^j}\{V^j(x)-p\cdot x\}=\min_{x \in X^j}\{p\cdot x+(-V^j(x))\}
    \]
    is precisely a tropical polynomial (in the min-plus convention),
    and its tropical hypersurface is precisely the LIP. In particular,
    \[
        -U_j(p)=\bigoplus_{x \in X^j}(-V^j(x))x^p
    \]
    in tropical arithmetic. 
    
    The demand complex is the corresponding regular subdivision
    of the convex hull of the bundles, analogous to the
    subdivision of a Newton polytope of a tropical polynomial.
\end{rmk}



\subsection{Demand Types}

The duality above shows that the directions of edges in
the demand complex determine the normal directions of
facets in the LIP.

These directions describe how an agent's demanded bundle
can change when prices change.

\begin{defin}
    Let $\mathcal D\subseteq\zz^n$ be a set of nonzero
    primitive integer vectors, closed under negation.

    A valuation is of \textit{demand type} $\mathcal D$
    if every facet of its LIP has a normal vector
    belonging to $\mathcal D$.
\end{defin}

Equivalently, every edge of its demand complex is parallel
to some vector in $\mathcal D$.

Note that a valuation may belong to multiple demand types.
The smallest such set, consisting of all primitive facet
normal directions and their negatives, is its
\textit{minimal demand type}.

\begin{example}
    Recall our two agents.

    Sunny's demand complex is a triangle, with edge
    directions
    \[
        (1,0),\quad (0,1),\quad (1,-1).
    \]
    Hence his minimal demand type is
    \[
        \mathcal D_S=\pm\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,-1)\}.
    \]

    The direction $(1,-1)$ represents substituting one
    room for the other.

    Rainy's demand complex is a line segment with
    direction $(1,1)$. Her minimal demand type is
    \[
        \mathcal D_R=\pm\{(1,1)\}.
    \]

    This direction represents acquiring or giving up
    both rooms together.
\end{example}

Demand types provide a geometric classification of
preferences: we retain the possible directions of demand
changes, rather than the exact valuations or prices.

Two particularly important classes are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ordinary substitutes:} primitive directions
    with at most one positive and at most one negative
    coordinate.
    \item \textbf{Strong substitutes:} directions of the form
    $\pm e_i$ or $e_i-e_j$, where $e_i$ denotes the
    $i$-th standard basis vector.
\end{itemize}

\begin{rmk}
    The strong-substitutes demand type is a subset of the
    ordinary-substitutes demand type. They coincide when
    each good has at most one available unit.
    
    The usefulness of demand types becomes apparent when
    we combine multiple agents. In particular, certain
    collections of demand directions guarantee the existence
    of competitive equilibrium. 
\end{rmk}
