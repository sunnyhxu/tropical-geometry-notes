\section{Motivations}

Our goal is to use geometry to visualize bidders' preferences.
The following example illustrates how a bidder's demand changes
across price space.

\begin{example} \label{graph:sub-comp}
    A hotel offers two rooms, labeled 1 and 2, at prices $p_1$ and $p_2$,
    respectively. Sunny wants at most one room. His utility at the possible
    bundles is
    \[
        U_{\text{Sunny}}(0,0)=0,\quad
        U_{\text{Sunny}}(1,0)=30-p_1,\quad
        U_{\text{Sunny}}(0,1)=40-p_2.
    \]

    Thus, Sunny's demand is determined by the largest of the three affine
    functions $0$, $30-p_1$, and $40-p_2$. Figure~\ref{fig:sunny-demand}
    shows the resulting subdivision of the nonnegative price orthant.

    \SunnyDemandFigure

    Note that the two rooms are substitutes for Sunny.

    Rainy, by contrast, wants either both rooms or neither room. Her utility is
    \[
        U_{\text{Rainy}}(0,0)=0,\quad
        U_{\text{Rainy}}(1,1)=50-p_1-p_2.
    \]

    Rainy buys both rooms precisely when $p_1+p_2<50$ and buys neither
    when $p_1+p_2>50$. Her demand is therefore separated by a single
    indifference line, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rainy-demand}.

    \RainyDemandFigure

    Note that the two rooms are complements for Rainy.
\end{example}

We say that a \textit{competitive equilibrium} exists at a price vector $p$ if
the entire supply can be allocated so that every agent receives a
utility-maximizing bundle at $p$.

\begin{example}
    We can visualize Sunny's and Rainy's demands in
    Example~\ref{graph:sub-comp} on a single graph by superposing their demand
    complexes, as in Figure~\ref{fig:combined-demand}.

    \CombinedDemandFigure
    
    Since the supply is one copy of each room, $s=(1,1)$, market clearing
    would require Rainy to receive both rooms and Sunny to receive neither.
    The two shaded demand regions do not intersect, so no competitive
    equilibrium exists.

    Now suppose that Rainy values the pair of rooms more highly. Her updated
    utility is
    \[
        U'_{\text{Rainy}}(0,0)=0,\quad
        U'_{\text{Rainy}}(1,1)=80-p_1-p_2.
    \]

    Figure~\ref{fig:updated-combined-demand} retains Rainy's original
    indifference line as a dashed reference and adds her updated indifference
    line.

    \UpdatedCombinedDemandFigure

    A competitive equilibrium exists at every price vector in the shaded
    triangle. Indeed, this region is defined by
    $p_1\ge 30$, $p_2\ge 40$, and $p_1+p_2\le 80$: Sunny demands no room,
    while Rainy demands both.
\end{example}

The rest of these notes develops the geometry underlying these pictures.
We first study the geometry generated by a single agent, then understand
how these geometries combine across agents, and finally use this structure
to study existence and counting of competitive equilibria.

\section{Demand Geometry of a Single Agent}

\subsection{Locus of indifference prices}

Let $J$ be the set of agents. For each $j \in J$, let
$X^j \subseteq \zz^n$ be the set of bundles available to agent $j$. The
agent's valuation is a function $V^j\colon X^j \to \rr$, and the corresponding
quasilinear utility of bundle $x$ at prices $p\in\rr^n$ is
$V^j(x)-p\cdot x$. Thus, agent $j$'s demand correspondence is
\[
    D^j(p)=\argmax_{x \in X^j}\{V^j(x)-p\cdot x\}.
\]

For a fixed bundle $x$, the function $V^j(x)-p\cdot x$ is affine-linear in
$p$. Consequently, price space is subdivided into regions according to which
of these affine functions attain the maximum.

In this notation, competitive equilibrium can be defined as follows.

\begin{defin}
    Given a supply vector $s$, a \textit{competitive equilibrium} consists of
    a price vector $p$ and bundles $(x^j)_{j\in J}$ such that
    \[
        x^j \in D^j(p) \quad\text{for every } j \in J,
        \qquad
        \sum_{j \in J}x^j=s.
    \]
\end{defin}

\begin{defin}
    The \textit{locus of indifference prices} (LIP) for agent $j$ is the set
    of prices at which the agent demands more than one bundle:
    \[
        L^j = \{p \in \rr^n : |D^j(p)|>1\}.
    \]

    The dark linear pieces in Figures~\ref{fig:sunny-demand}
    and~\ref{fig:rainy-demand} are the corresponding loci of indifference
    prices.
\end{defin}
